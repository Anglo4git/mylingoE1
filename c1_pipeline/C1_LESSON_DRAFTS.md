## G1. Uses of continuous tenses
**Objective:** Use continuous aspect across tenses for its C1-level
nuances — temporariness, incompleteness, emotional colouring, and
politeness softening — beyond the basic present/past continuous already
taught at lower levels.
**Explanation:** Continuous aspect at C1 signals more than "in progress":
it can soften a statement ("I was hoping you could help"), suggest
temporariness vs. permanence ("She's living in Paris" vs. "She lives in
Paris"), or add emotional colour to repeated actions (see B2's "always +
continuous" for the annoyance use — this lesson covers the wider family of
nuances beyond that one case).
**Examples:**
- "I was wondering if you'd be free Thursday." (softened request)
- "He's always been sending me links to weird articles." (present perfect
  continuous, emphasising an ongoing pattern)
- "We're flying to Rome next week." (continuous for a fixed future plan —
  contrast with the simple/going-to forms covered at B2)
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 direct statements/requests using continuous aspect
to soften or add nuance.
**Quiz link:** tests recognising the nuance (softening/temporariness/
emotional colour), not basic tense formation.

## G2. Uses of would
**Objective:** Use "would" across its full range — past habits, polite
requests, hypothetical meaning, refusal/insistence in the past, and
reported future — distinguishing them in context.
**Explanation:** "Would" has several distinct C1-level uses beyond the
B2-level past-habit meaning: hypothetical/conditional ("I would help if I
could"), polite distancing ("Would you mind...?"), refusal in the past
("The car wouldn't start"), and reported future ("She said she would
call").
**Examples:**
- "He wouldn't listen no matter what I said." (refusal/insistence)
- "I would think that's the right approach." (hedged opinion, not habit)
- "She said she would call once she landed." (reported future)
**Practice:** Identify which of the 5 uses "would" has in 5 given
sentences.
**Quiz link:** tests distinguishing uses of "would" beyond the past-habit
meaning already covered at B2.

## G3. Articles
**Objective:** Use articles correctly in the trickier C1-level cases:
generic reference, abstract/uncountable nouns, and fixed
expressions/idioms where article use doesn't follow the basic rules.
**Explanation:** Generic reference can use a/an, the, or no article
depending on register ("A tiger is a dangerous animal" / "The tiger is an
endangered species" / "Tigers are dangerous"). Abstract nouns often take no
article ("Life is unpredictable") but can take "a/the" when specified
("a life well lived"). Fixed expressions often break the rules ("in
hospital" vs. "in the hospital," "go to school" vs. "go to the school").
**Examples:**
- "The internet has changed how we communicate." (generic, "the" + noun)
- "He's in hospital after the accident." (British fixed expression, no
  article)
- "It was a life-changing experience." (abstract noun, specified with
  "a")
**Practice:** Fill 5 gaps with a/an/the/no article, focusing on the
trickier generic and fixed-expression cases.
**Quiz link:** tests the exception cases, not basic a/an/the rules assumed
already known from earlier levels.

## G4. Past Perfect
**Objective:** Use past perfect (simple and continuous) precisely in
extended narrative and to signal subtle sequencing/causality that past
simple alone can't convey.
**Explanation:** Beyond the basic "earlier past event" rule (already
covered at B1/B2 alongside past simple/continuous), C1 focuses on: past
perfect in reported speech and hypotheticals (already tense-shifted
contexts), past perfect continuous for cause ("He was tired because he'd
been working all night"), and stylistic choices in narrative writing where
past perfect signals a flashback.
**Examples:**
- "By the time the ambulance arrived, the situation had already
  changed dramatically." (narrative flashback marker)
- "She was exhausted — she'd been studying for twelve hours straight."
  (continuous, cause)
- "If only I had known." (hypothetical regret, connects to B2's
  wish/if only)
**Practice:** Rewrite a short narrative paragraph inserting past perfect
where it clarifies sequence.
**Quiz link:** tests using past perfect for narrative/stylistic effect, not
the basic sequencing rule.

## G5. Order of adverbs
**Objective:** Order multiple adverbs/adverbials correctly when several
appear in the same sentence (manner, place, frequency, time).
**Explanation:** Standard order: manner, place, frequency, time (though
frequency adverbs of the "always/often/usually" type go before the main
verb, not at the end). When several end-position adverbials combine, the
order is usually manner → place → time.
**Examples:**
- "She sang beautifully at the concert last night." (manner, place, time)
- "He works out regularly at the gym." (frequency-type adverb placement
  differs from end-position frequency phrases like "every day")
- "They quietly left the room a few minutes ago." (manner before verb
  phrase, time at the end)
**Practice:** Reorder 4 scrambled sentences with multiple adverbials into
correct order.
**Quiz link:** tests ordering multiple adverbials together, not single
adverb placement (assumed known from B2's "Adverbs of manner and
modifiers").

## G6. Order of adjectives
**Objective:** Order multiple adjectives before a noun correctly using the
standard sequence.
**Explanation:** Standard order: opinion → size → age → shape → colour →
origin → material → purpose + noun. Commas separate adjectives of the same
type; no comma between different types.
**Examples:**
- "a beautiful small old round wooden Italian dining table" (extreme
  example showing the full order)
- "an interesting new French film" (opinion, age, origin)
- "a small round table," not "a round small table"
**Practice:** Reorder 4 sets of scrambled adjectives into the correct
sequence before a given noun.
**Quiz link:** tests the standard ordering sequence across several
adjective-type combinations.

## G9. Ellipsis and elision
⚠ Note: `c1-006` "Ellipsis" is already live. This lesson is scoped to the
"elision" half specifically (and any ellipsis sub-points not already
covered), per the human's deep-dive-not-repeat instruction.
**Objective:** Recognise and use elision (the omission of sounds in
connected speech) and any remaining ellipsis patterns not already covered
by the existing Ellipsis lesson.
**Explanation:** Ellipsis = omitting words recoverable from context ("Want

"han'bag"). This lesson focuses on elision as the newer half of the topic.
**Examples:**
- "I don't know" → naturally reduced to something like "I dunno" in fast
  speech.
- "Could've" (elided form of "could have") in casual speech and informal
  writing.
**Practice:** Identify 4 examples of elision in transcribed casual speech
and explain what's been omitted.
**Quiz link:** tests recognising elision specifically, kept distinct from
the already-live ellipsis content.

## G10. Question tags
⚠ Unblocked per the human's spiral-curriculum decision (see
C1_CURRICULUM_ISSUES.md, cross-level overlap issue 13). This lesson
assumes B1's basic tags and B2's special-case tags (imperatives, "Let's,"
"I am → aren't I") are already known, and focuses on intonation-driven
meaning and register.
**Objective:** Use question tag intonation to distinguish a genuine
question from a rhetorical/confirming statement, and use tags
appropriately in formal register.
**Explanation:** Rising intonation on a tag = genuine question, unsure of
the answer. Falling intonation = seeking agreement/confirmation, not
really asking. In formal register, tags are often avoided in favour of
other confirmation-seeking phrases ("wouldn't you agree?" or restructuring
the sentence).
**Examples:**
- "You're coming, aren't you?" (falling intonation — assumes yes)
- "You're coming, aren't you?" (rising intonation — genuinely unsure)
- Formal alternative: "This approach seems most effective, wouldn't you
  agree?"
**Practice:** Mark 4 question-tag sentences as "genuine question" or
"seeking agreement" based on context, then rewrite one in formal register
without a tag.
**Quiz link:** tests intonation-meaning and formal-register awareness, not
tag formation (assumed known).

## G11. Future Continuous
**Objective:** Use future continuous for its C1-level nuances — polite
enquiry about others' plans, and contrast with future simple/going to for
predicted routine vs. decision.
**Explanation:** Future continuous ("I'll be seeing him tomorrow") often
sounds more casual/assumed than future simple, and is used to politely ask
about someone's plans without seeming to make a request ("Will you be
using the car later?" vs. the more direct "Can I use the car later?").
**Examples:**
- "Will you be passing the post office? Could you drop this off?" (polite,
  indirect request via future continuous)
- "I'll be working late again tonight." (predicted routine, assumed)
- Contrast: "I'll work late tonight" (decision) vs. "I'll be working late
  tonight" (assumed routine/part of a plan already in motion)
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 direct requests as polite indirect ones using
future continuous.
**Quiz link:** tests the polite-enquiry and assumed-routine uses
specifically, distinguishing them from future simple.

⚠ Unblocked per the human's spiral-curriculum decision (see

**Objective:** Use formal prepositional relative clause patterns

  delayed for."
**Practice:** Rewrite 4 neutral-register relative clauses (preposition
stranded) into formal register (preposition fronted).

not defining/non-defining basics (assumed known from B2).

## G14. Passive
⚠ Unblocked per the human's spiral-curriculum decision (see
C1_CURRICULUM_ISSUES.md, cross-level overlap issue 14). Assumes B2's
foundational passive (`b2-020`) and Advanced Passive (`b2-006`) are
already known. C1 focus: passive of reporting/belief verbs and the
causative-passive-adjacent "get" passive.
**Objective:** Use passive reporting structures ("It is said that...," "He
is believed to have...") and the informal "get" passive appropriately.
**Explanation:** Passive reporting structures distance the speaker from a
claim's source, common in news/academic writing: "It is thought that..." /
"She is understood to have resigned." The "get" passive is more informal
than "be" passive and often implies something happening to someone,
sometimes unexpectedly: "He got promoted" / "The window got broken."
**Examples:**
- "It is widely believed that the policy will be reversed."
- "The suspect is alleged to have left the country."
- "My bike got stolen last week." (informal, "get" passive)
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 direct-attribution sentences ("People say that...")
as passive reporting structures, then rewrite 2 "be" passive sentences
using "get" passive where appropriate (informal contexts only).
**Quiz link:** tests reporting-passive structures and get-passive register,
not basic passive formation (assumed known from B2).

## G16. Reported speech
⚠ Unblocked per the human's spiral-curriculum decision (see
C1_CURRICULUM_ISSUES.md, cross-level overlap issue 15). Assumes B1 basics
and B2's extended reporting-verb patterns (`b2-022`) are already known. C1
focus: reporting speech without a reporting verb (free indirect style) and
reporting nuanced attitude.
**Objective:** Recognise and use free indirect speech (blending reported
and narrative voice without "he said that...") and report a speaker's
attitude, not just their words.
**Explanation:** Free indirect style keeps the tense-shift of reported
speech but drops the reporting clause, common in narrative writing: "She
would be there tomorrow, she was sure of it" (no "she said/thought that").
Reporting attitude uses verb choice to convey how something was said:
"muttered," "insisted," "conceded," "admitted" (some already covered at
B2's reporting-verb lesson; this extends the range further).
**Examples:**
- "He wasn't coming. That much was certain." (free indirect, no reporting
  clause)
- "She conceded that he had a point." (attitude: reluctant agreement)
- "He muttered that it wasn't his problem." (attitude: irritation)
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 direct-speech lines as free indirect speech, and 2
as reported speech choosing a reporting verb that conveys a specified
attitude.
**Quiz link:** tests free indirect style and attitude-conveying reporting
verbs, not the reporting-verb patterns already covered at B2.
