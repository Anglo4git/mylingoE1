## G1. Habit in the Present and the Past
⚠ Flag (Issue E, not resolved): overlaps with B1 "Used to" and with G16 below.
Drafted as a standalone lesson; framing may need to change once merged/kept-
separate is decided.
**Objective:** Describe repeated/habitual actions in the present and past using
a range of structures, not just "usually."
**Explanation:** Present habits: present simple ("I walk to work"), "tend to,"
"have a habit of + -ing." Past habits: "used to," "would" (only for repeated
actions, not states), past simple.
**Examples:**
- "She tends to check her phone first thing in the morning."
- "We used to spend every summer at my grandmother's."
- "As a kid, he would climb that tree every afternoon." (not: *he would live
  there — "would" doesn't work for past states)
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 present-simple habit sentences using "tend to" or "have
a habit of."
**Quiz link:** tests present-habit structures, would vs used to, and the
would-for-states restriction.

## G2. Present Perfect Simple and Present Perfect Continuous
**Objective:** Choose between simple and continuous present perfect based on
whether the focus is on result/completion or on duration/ongoing activity.
**Explanation:** Simple = completed action, result matters ("I've finished the
report"). Continuous = emphasis on duration or an activity still in progress
("I've been working on it all morning"). Some verbs (know, believe, own) rarely
take continuous.
**Examples:**
- "I've written three emails this morning." (result: 3 emails exist)
- "I've been writing emails all morning." (duration, possibly still going)
- "She's known him for ten years." (not *has been knowing)
**Practice:** Given 4 short contexts, choose simple or continuous and justify
in one sentence.
**Quiz link:** tests the result-vs-duration distinction and stative-verb
restriction.

## G3. Past Simple, Past Continuous, and Past Perfect
**Objective:** Sequence past events correctly using all three past tenses
together.
**Explanation:** Past simple = the main past event. Past continuous = background/
interrupted action. Past perfect = further back than another past event.
**Examples:**
- "I was cooking dinner when the phone rang." (continuous + simple)
- "By the time she arrived, we had already eaten." (perfect further back)
- "He left the party early because he had drunk too much." (perfect explains
  cause before the simple-past result)
**Practice:** Put a 5-sentence jumbled story in the correct tense sequence.
**Quiz link:** tests choosing the right tense for the right point in a timeline.

## G4. Question tags
**Objective:** Form and use question tags correctly, including for imperatives
and less common auxiliaries.
**Explanation:** Positive statement → negative tag, and vice versa. Match the
auxiliary/modal of the main clause. Special cases: "I am" → "aren't I?";
imperatives → "..., will you?"; "Let's..." → "..., shall we?"
**Examples:**
- "You haven't seen my keys, have you?"
- "Close the door, will you?"
- "Let's get some coffee, shall we?"
**Practice:** Add the correct tag to 5 statements, including one imperative and
one "Let's" sentence.
**Quiz link:** tests auxiliary matching and the special-case tags.

## G5. Will, going to, Present Simple, Present Continuous for the future
**Objective:** Choose the right future form for spontaneous decisions, plans,
schedules, and fixed arrangements.
**Explanation:** Will = spontaneous decision/prediction without evidence. Going
to = intention/plan, or prediction with present evidence. Present continuous =
fixed arrangement (usually with a time/place). Present simple = timetable/
schedule, not personal plans.
**Examples:**
- "I'll get that — don't worry." (spontaneous)
- "Look at those clouds — it's going to rain." (evidence)
- "I'm meeting Sara at six." (arrangement)
- "The train leaves at 9:15." (timetable)
**Practice:** Match 4 future sentences to the reason (spontaneous / plan /
arrangement / timetable).
**Quiz link:** tests distinguishing all four forms in context.

## G6. Future Perfect
**Objective:** Describe an action that will be completed before a specific
future point.
**Explanation:** "will have + past participle," often with "by [time]." Can
combine with continuous for duration: "will have been [-ing] for..."
**Examples:**
- "By next June, I will have graduated."
- "By the time you arrive, we'll have finished dinner."
- "In March, she'll have been working here for five years."
**Practice:** Complete 4 sentences with "by [time], ... will have ..."
**Quiz link:** tests forming future perfect and choosing it over will/going to
when a deadline is implied.

## G7. Phrasal verbs (grammar pattern)
⚠ Note: this is the grammar-pattern angle (separable vs inseparable, transitive
vs intransitive). The vocabulary/lexical list of phrasal verbs is a separate
item (V6) — see Issue 5 in B2_CURRICULUM_ISSUES.md, not merged here.
**Objective:** Recognise and correctly position the object with separable vs
inseparable phrasal verbs.
**Explanation:** Separable: object can go between or after ("turn the TV off" /
"turn off the TV," but pronoun must go between: "turn it off," not *turn off
it). Inseparable: object must follow ("look after the kids," not *look the
kids after). Some are three-part and always inseparable ("look forward to").
**Examples:**
- "She turned down the offer." / "She turned it down."
- "Who's looking after the dog this weekend?"
- "I'm looking forward to the trip."
**Practice:** Rewrite 4 sentences replacing the object with a pronoun, fixing
word order where needed.
**Quiz link:** tests separable/inseparable placement, not individual verb
meanings (that's V6).

## G8. Zero, first, second and third conditionals
⚠ Flag (Issue H, NOT resolved): `b2-001` "Mixed Conditionals" is already live
and goes beyond the four basic types. This draft deliberately stays within the
four standard types only and does not touch mixed forms, to avoid contradicting
or duplicating b2-001 before the human resolves Issue H. If the human decides
mixed conditionals are in scope for this item too, this lesson needs revising.
**Objective:** Form and choose correctly between the four standard conditional
types.
**Explanation:** Zero = general truths (if + present, present). First = real
future possibility (if + present, will). Second = unreal/unlikely present (if +
past, would). Third = unreal past (if + past perfect, would have + p.p.).
**Examples:**
- "If you heat water to 100°C, it boils." (zero)
- "If it rains tomorrow, we'll cancel." (first)
- "If I won the lottery, I'd travel the world." (second)
- "If she had left earlier, she wouldn't have missed the train." (third)
**Practice:** Identify the conditional type in 4 given sentences, then write
one of each type about yourself.
**Quiz link:** tests correctly identifying/forming each of the four types.

## G9. Wish and if only
**Objective:** Express regret, dissatisfaction, and desire for change using
"wish" and "if only" with the correct tense.
**Explanation:** Wish/if only + past simple = present regret/desire for
change. Wish/if only + past perfect = past regret. Wish + would = frustration
about another person's/thing's behaviour (not about yourself).
**Examples:**
- "I wish I lived closer to my family." (present)
- "If only I had studied medicine." (past regret)
- "I wish you would stop interrupting me." (frustration, not *I wish I would)
**Practice:** Complete 4 sentences choosing the correct tense for the intended
meaning (present regret / past regret / frustration).
**Quiz link:** tests the three tense/meaning combinations, especially the
"wish + would" restriction.

## G10. Passive
⚠ Flag (Issue I-adjacent, NOT resolved): `b2-006` "Advanced Passive" is
already live. This draft covers foundational passive use (why/when to use it,
basic tense passives) rather than the advanced forms (passive of reporting
verbs, causative-style passives) that b2-006 likely already covers, to avoid
duplicating it. Human should confirm this split is wanted, per the curriculum
map's note.
**Objective:** Choose active or passive appropriately and form the passive
across common tenses.
**Explanation:** Use passive when the agent is unknown, unimportant, or
obvious, or to keep focus on the object/result — common in formal/academic/news
writing. Form: be + past participle, tense carried by "be."
**Examples:**
- "The bridge was built in 1889." (agent unknown/unimportant)
- "Mistakes were made." (avoiding blame/formal distance)
- "The results are being analysed." (present continuous passive)
**Practice:** Rewrite 4 active sentences as passive, choosing whether "by..."
is needed.
**Quiz link:** tests when passive is the better choice and basic tense
formation, not the advanced forms already covered by b2-006.

## G11. Compounds of some, any, no, every
**Objective:** Use compounds (someone/anyone/no one/everyone, etc.) correctly
in statements, questions, and negatives.
**Explanation:** Some- = affirmative statements / polite offers in questions.
Any- = negatives and most questions. No- = negative meaning without "not."
Every- = all, universally.
**Examples:**
- "Someone left this here." / "Would you like something to drink?" (offer)
- "Is anybody home?" / "I didn't see anyone."
- "Nothing surprises me anymore." (not *I don't know nothing — no double
  negative)
- "Everywhere I go, I see the same ad."
**Practice:** Fill 5 gaps choosing the correct some-/any-/no-/every- compound.
**Quiz link:** tests the affirmative/negative/question distribution and the
no-double-negative rule.

## G12. Reported speech
⚠ Flag (Issue A, NOT resolved): B1 already has reported speech (authored,

## G13. Relative clauses
⚠ Flag (Issue A, NOT resolved): B1 has authored-but-unshipped relative-clause
content; nothing is live at either level yet. This draft doesn't need to avoid
duplicating live B1 content, but the human should be aware both levels are
about to have relative-clause lessons and confirm the B1/B2 split (basic
defining clauses at B1 vs non-defining + reduction at B2, as assumed here).
**Objective:** Use defining and non-defining relative clauses correctly,
including reduced forms.
**Explanation:** Defining = essential info, no commas ("The man who called is
my uncle"). Non-defining = extra info, commas required, cannot use "that"
("My uncle, who called this morning, is a doctor"). Reduced: drop
relative pronoun + be in some defining clauses ("The book [that was] left on
the table is mine").
**Examples:**
- "The woman who lives next door is a doctor." (defining)
- "Paris, which I visited last year, was beautiful." (non-defining, commas
  required)
- "The man standing by the door is my brother." (reduced from "who is
  standing")
**Practice:** Combine 4 sentence pairs into one using the correct relative
clause type, with/without commas.
**Quiz link:** tests defining vs non-defining punctuation and pronoun choice
("that" never in non-defining clauses).

## G14. Conjunctions: although, despite, in spite of, otherwise, unless
⚠ Flag (Issue K, NOT resolved): overlaps with the already-live "Concession"
(b2-009) and with functional item F9 "Stating contrast." Drafted here at the
level of basic connector words and their grammar (despite/in spite of + noun
or -ing, vs although + clause) — not advanced concessive structures, which are
assumed to already be b2-009's territory. Human should confirm this split.
**Objective:** Use contrast/condition conjunctions with the correct following
grammar.
**Explanation:** Although/even though + clause. Despite/in spite of + noun or
-ing (never + clause directly — common error). Otherwise = "if not" consequence.
Unless = "if not" condition.
**Examples:**
- "Although it was raining, we went for a walk."
- "Despite the rain, we went for a walk." (not *despite it was raining)
- "Hurry up, otherwise we'll miss the train."
- "I won't go unless you come with me."
**Practice:** Fill 5 gaps choosing the right conjunction and fixing the
following grammar (noun/-ing vs clause).
**Quiz link:** tests the despite/in spite of + noun-or-ing vs although +
clause distinction, plus otherwise vs unless meaning.

## G15. Modals: present and perfect
⚠ Flag (Issue I, NOT resolved): only the "present modals" half is drafted here.
Perfect modals (deduction/regret) are already live as b2-003 "Modal Perfect" —
this deliberately does not re-author that half, to avoid duplication. Human
should confirm the narrow "deduction/regret only" scope already shipped in
b2-003 is intentional and complete.
**Objective:** Use present modals precisely for ability, permission, and
obligation, including negative-obligation nuance.
**Explanation:** Must/have to = obligation (must = personal/speaker's
authority; have to = external rule). Mustn't = prohibition. Don't have to /
needn't = no obligation, not prohibition — a common learner error.
**Examples:**
- "You must call your mother back." (personal urging)
- "I have to submit this by Friday." (external deadline)
- "You mustn't park here." (prohibited)
- "You don't have to come if you're tired." (optional, not forbidden)
**Practice:** 5 sentences: choose must/have to/mustn't/don't have to for the
given meaning.
**Quiz link:** tests the don't-have-to-vs-mustn't distinction specifically,
since that's the highest-risk confusion at this level.

## G16. Always for frequency + present continuous
⚠ Flag (Issue E, NOT resolved): overlaps with G1 above. Drafted as a narrow,
distinct point (the emphatic/annoyance use only) rather than general habits, to
minimise overlap until the human decides whether to merge.
**Objective:** Use "always + present continuous" to express annoyance or
surprise about a repeated behaviour — distinct from neutral habitual present
simple.
**Explanation:** Present simple + always = neutral fact. Present continuous +
always = emphatic, usually irritated or surprised tone.
**Examples:**
- "He always forgets his keys." (neutral fact)
- "He's always forgetting his keys!" (exasperated)
- "She's always coming up with new ideas." (impressed/surprised, not neutral)
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 neutral "always + present simple" sentences to add an
annoyed or impressed tone using the continuous form.
**Quiz link:** tests recognising the tone shift, not just the grammatical form.

## G17. Adverbs of manner and modifiers
⚠ Flag (Issue 7 from the seed, NOT resolved): same phrasing appears at A2/B1.
Drafted as a B2-level deepening (position rules with different verb types,
modifying adjectives/other adverbs) rather than basic -ly formation, which is
assumed already taught. Human should confirm this isn't a repeat.
**Objective:** Position adverbs of manner correctly and use modifying adverbs
(intensifiers/downtoners) with adjectives and other adverbs.
**Explanation:** Manner adverbs usually go after the verb/object, not between
verb and object. Modifiers (extremely, fairly, slightly, remarkably) go before
the adjective/adverb they modify, and some pair more naturally with gradable
vs ungradable adjectives (very tired, but absolutely exhausted, not *very
exhausted).
**Examples:**
- "She answered the question calmly." (not *she calmly answered the question
  — both possible, but not *answered calmly the question)
- "It's fairly warm today." / "It was absolutely freezing." (ungradable →
  absolutely, not very)
**Practice:** Fix word order in 3 sentences and choose the correct modifier
(very/absolutely) for 3 adjectives marked gradable or ungradable.
**Quiz link:** tests adverb placement and gradable/ungradable modifier
pairing.

## G18. Participle adjectives
⚠ Flag (Issue J, NOT resolved): distinct from the already-live "Participle
Clauses" (b2-005) — this covers -ed/-ing adjectives (bored/boring), not reduced
relative/adverbial clauses. Human should confirm both are meant to coexist as
separate lessons.
**Objective:** Choose correctly between -ed and -ing adjective forms.
**Explanation:** -ed = describes how someone feels (the experiencer). -ing =
describes the thing/person causing the feeling.
**Examples:**
- "I'm bored." (how I feel) vs "This film is boring." (what causes it)
- "She was fascinated by the lecture." / "The lecture was fascinating."
- "He felt embarrassed." / "That was an embarrassing mistake."
**Practice:** Choose -ed or -ing for 5 gapped sentences based on context.
**Quiz link:** tests the experiencer-vs-cause distinction across several
common pairs.

## F1. Giving opinions
**Objective:** Give and support opinions with appropriate hedging/strength.
**Explanation:** Strong: "I'm convinced that..." Neutral: "I think/believe..."
Tentative: "I'd say..." / "It seems to me that..."
**Examples:**
- "I'm convinced that remote work improves productivity."
- "It seems to me that the plan needs more detail."
**Practice:** Rephrase 3 blunt opinions using a softer or stronger opener as
specified.
**Quiz link:** tests matching opener strength to intended tone.

## F2. Summarising
**Objective:** Summarise a longer point concisely using standard signal
phrases.
**Explanation:** "In short...," "To sum up...," "The main point is...," "So
basically..."
**Examples:**
- "To sum up, the project is behind schedule but on budget."
- "So basically, we need more time."
**Practice:** Write a one-sentence summary of a given short paragraph using a
signal phrase.
**Quiz link:** tests recognising and producing summary language.

## F3. Expressing regret
**Objective:** Express regret about past actions/decisions appropriately.
**Explanation:** "I regret + -ing/that...," "I wish I hadn't...," "If only I
had..." (see also G9 for the grammar; this item focuses on the functional
phrases used in conversation, not the full tense system).
**Examples:**
- "I regret not applying for that job."
- "I wish I hadn't said that."
**Practice:** Respond to 3 scenarios expressing regret appropriately.
**Quiz link:** tests phrase choice in context, complementing G9's grammar
focus without repeating it.

## F4. Drawing conclusions
**Objective:** Signal a logical conclusion from evidence or discussion.
**Explanation:** "It follows that...," "This suggests that...," "Therefore...,"
"All things considered..."
**Examples:**
- "Sales dropped in every region. This suggests the issue isn't local."
- "All things considered, the trip was a success."
**Practice:** Add an appropriate conclusion phrase to 3 short arguments.
**Quiz link:** tests recognising which conclusion phrase fits the evidence
given.

## F5. Making offers
**Objective:** Make offers with appropriate formality.
**Explanation:** Informal: "Want me to...?" / "I'll..." Neutral: "Shall I...?"
Formal: "Would you like me to...?" / "I'd be happy to..."
**Examples:**
- "Shall I grab you a coffee?"
- "I'd be happy to review the document for you."
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 offers at a different formality level than given.
**Quiz link:** tests matching offer phrasing to formality level.

## F6. Describing cause and effect
**Objective:** Link causes and effects using a range of connectors beyond
"because."
**Explanation:** "Due to," "as a result of," "this led to," "consequently,"
"owing to."
**Examples:**
- "Due to the strike, flights were delayed."
- "The price rose, and consequently demand fell."
**Practice:** Combine 4 cause/effect sentence pairs using a specified
connector.
**Quiz link:** tests connector choice and required following grammar (noun vs
clause).

## F7. Stating purpose
**Objective:** State the purpose of an action clearly, with a range of
structures.
**Explanation:** "In order to...," "so as to...," "with the aim of + -ing,"
"the purpose/point of X is to..."
**Examples:**
- "We met in order to finalise the budget."
- "The workshop's aim is to improve communication skills."
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 "because I wanted to..." sentences using a
purpose-structure instead.
**Quiz link:** tests forming purpose structures correctly (infinitive vs
-ing after "with the aim of").

## F8. Emphasising
**Objective:** Emphasise a point using stress structures and intensifying
phrases.
**Explanation:** Cleft sentences: "What really matters is...," "It was Sara
who...". Intensifiers: "absolutely," "the fact is," "I can't stress enough
that..."
**Examples:**
- "What really matters is that everyone's safe."
- "It was Sara who first raised the issue."
**Practice:** Rewrite 3 plain statements as cleft sentences for emphasis.
**Quiz link:** tests forming cleft sentences ("What.../It was...who...").

## F9. Stating contrast
⚠ Flag (Issue K, NOT resolved): overlap risk with G14 (Conjunctions) and the
already-live "Concession" (b2-009). Drafted here as conversational/phrase-level
contrast markers, not grammar-level conjunction rules (G14's territory).
**Objective:** Use conversational phrases to contrast two ideas or opinions.
**Explanation:** "On the other hand...," "Then again...," "Having said
that...," "That said..."
**Examples:**
- "The job pays well. Then again, the commute is brutal."
- "It's expensive. Having said that, the quality is excellent."
**Practice:** Add an appropriate contrast phrase to 3 two-part statements.
**Quiz link:** tests conversational contrast phrases, kept distinct from G14's
conjunction-grammar focus.

## F10. Adding information
**Objective:** Add supporting information smoothly using a range of connectors.
**Explanation:** "What's more...," "In addition to that...," "Not only... but
also...," "On top of that..."
**Examples:**
- "The flat is cheap. What's more, it's close to the station."
- "Not only did she finish early, but she also helped others."
**Practice:** Combine 3 sentence pairs using a specified addition connector,
adjusting word order for "not only" inversion.
**Quiz link:** tests connector choice and the inversion rule after "not only"
at the start of a clause.

## F11. Congratulating
**Objective:** Congratulate someone appropriately across formal and informal
contexts.
**Explanation:** Informal: "Nice one!" / "Way to go!" Neutral/formal:
"Congratulations on...," "Well done on..."
**Examples:**
- "Congratulations on your promotion!"
- "Nice one, you totally nailed that presentation."
**Practice:** Write a congratulatory response to 3 scenarios at the specified
formality.
**Quiz link:** tests matching phrase to context/formality.

## F12. Commiserating
**Objective:** Express sympathy for someone's bad news appropriately.
**Explanation:** "I'm so sorry to hear that," "That's rough," "What a shame,"
"You must be devastated."
**Examples:**
- "I'm so sorry to hear about the job — that's rough."
- "What a shame you couldn't make it."
**Practice:** Write a commiserating response to 3 scenarios, matching
seriousness of the news.
**Quiz link:** tests matching sympathy phrase to how serious the situation is.

## F13. Clarifying
**Objective:** Ask for and give clarification politely.
**Explanation:** Asking: "Sorry, could you clarify what you mean by...?" /
"When you say X, do you mean...?" Giving: "What I meant was...," "To put it
another way..."
**Examples:**
- "Sorry, could you clarify what you mean by 'soon'?"
- "What I meant was next week, not today."
**Practice:** Write a clarifying question and a clarifying response for 2
ambiguous statements.
**Quiz link:** tests forming polite clarification requests, not just
understanding meaning.

## F14. Guessing
**Objective:** Make tentative guesses using hedged language, at a wider range
than B1's guessing phrases.
**Explanation:** "I'd hazard a guess that...," "My best guess is...," "If I
had to guess, I'd say...," "It's anyone's guess."
**Examples:**
- "I'd hazard a guess that it'll cost around $200."
- "If I had to guess, I'd say she's in her thirties."
**Practice:** Respond to 3 uncertain scenarios using a specified guessing
phrase.
**Quiz link:** tests using more sophisticated hedging phrases than B1's basic
"I'd guess that/It could be."

## F15. Ordering arguments
**Objective:** Sequence points in an argument or explanation clearly.
**Explanation:** "First and foremost...," "Secondly...," "Furthermore...,"
"Finally...," "Last but not least..."
**Examples:**
- "First and foremost, safety must come first. Secondly, cost matters too."
- "Last but not least, we should consider the timeline."
**Practice:** Order 4 jumbled argument points using correct sequencing
phrases.
**Quiz link:** tests recognising/producing logical sequencing language.

## F16. Giving examples
**Objective:** Introduce examples smoothly within an argument or explanation.
**Explanation:** "For instance...," "A case in point is...," "To illustrate...,"
"Take X, for example."
**Examples:**
- "Several cities have banned plastic bags. Take France, for example."
- "A case in point is the 2019 policy change."
**Practice:** Add an example to 3 general statements using a specified phrase.
**Quiz link:** tests correct placement and phrasing of example-introducing
language.
